2. Brakes

2.1. Plumbing and installation

Brake lines were manufactured and installed onto the car. R26e utilizes 3AN stainless steel braided lines for its brake system. A combination of direct thread and banjo hose ends were used depending on the location and accessibility of the connection. The BOM is as follows:

The addition of dry ice cooling containers presented a unique challenge for routing the brake lines. The usual path to the rear of the chassis has been blocked off, and the rule that prevents routing under the chain still applies. The shutoff solenoid also had to be mounted in a location that is both well protected and easy to access for brakes bleeding, with the pressure sensor mounted further downstream instead of at the pedal box.

Hence, the brake lines were carefully laid out in CAD over multiple iterations to ensure no clashing with other components. A detailed brake line fabrication workflow will be illustrated in [Appendix 1] for reference.

For the brake rotors, brakes buttons and shims were used to secure them onto the hubs. The floating rotor system allows for outward thermal expansion when the rotor heats up. It also allows for small amounts of left-to-right movement of the rotors to account for the rigidly mounted callipers. However, there is no radial play of the rotors relative to the hubs, as it can cause excessive vibrations and lead to the eventual failure of the components.

In this use case, brake buttons should be treated as a wear item and inspected routinely for any irregularity. Should there be excessive play between the rotor and the hub, it could be a sign that the brake buttons have been eroded and therefore not fully supporting the rotor. The brake buttons should then be replaced as soon as possible. The previous season’s race car, R25e, covered a total of 400km before a noticeable rattle occurred within the brake system. It was found during inspection that the brake buttons were so worn out that the free play allowed them to contact the brake callipers. Fortunately, the incident only resulted in minor scratches, but it drives home the importance of routing inspections and maintenance.

Once the brakes were installed, hydraulics were bled and friction surfaces were degreased and bedded in to ensure optimal braking response. Brake temperature sensors monitored the heating of each rotor. This is to ensure that there are no anomalies such as excessive runout or stuck pistons, which can lead to elevated temperatures and premature failure.

2.2. Bedding

Brakes bedding was first performed to prepare the friction interface between the brake pads and brake rotors. This is achieved by applying consistent moderate braking pressure at high speed, and releasing the brakes before the car comes to a full stop. Friction material will be evenly transferred from the pads to the rotors and a change in colour of the brake rotor can be observed. Discolouration outside of the friction surface can also be observed, as the rotors turned to straw colour, indicating that the temperature of the rotors did reach approximately 220 C.

2.3. Shutoff valve and rege

R26e marks the first time in NUS FSAE where regenerative braking is being used to perform all of the braking on the rear axle. Previous implementations on R25e and R24e use a blend of hydraulic and regenerative braking. This is a simple and reliable approach, but the amount of energy recuperated through regen will be limited as the hydraulics can never be fully decoupled. With the shutoff valve installed, it is possible to cut off all fluid flow to the rear brakes and lean more heavily on regen. The logic to derive how much regen is needed is covered in [Appendix 2].

After some tuning, we can see that the energy recovered during regular braking events are greater on R26e compared to R25e. Over the course of 1 endurance stint, the amount of energy recovered is kWh.

Another feature of the new regen system is electronic brake bias. Previously, the driver had to interact with a physical cable in order to adjust brake balance. This method is prone to failure as the adjuster cable had a tendency to become jammed and prevent the balance bar from threading smoothly. With the implementation of the shutoff valve, it is possible to control brake bias via a knob on the dashboard, making the adjustment more consistent, more reliable as well as more tactile.



3. Hub bearings and preload

Wheel bearings were installed with grease on press fit interfaces as recommended by our bearings sponsor, NSK. This is to prevent corrosion and subsequent damage to the uprights and hubs. The fits between the wheel bearings, uprights and hubs are transitional fit (k5 and H6).

Another special consideration when using angular contact bearings is the preload. Angular contact bearings are almost exclusively used in tandem, and R26e uses a back-to-back configuration.

Preload refers to the assembly adjustment to the clearance of the bearing (typically negative clearance to keep the bearings stressed) [Reference 2]. Preload is important for maintaining rigidity during operation and ensuring that the rolling elements in the bearing do not slide.

To achieve preload, there are 2 common techniques:

1. Position preload. This technique involves the use of spacers on inner and outer races of the bearings, thus controlling the displacement between the inner and outer races.

2. Constant-pressure preload. This technique exerts pressure to push the inner race against the outer race by means of some spring elements.

The design of R26e’s upright falls under position preload, as it utilizes a step in the upright to act as the outer race “spacer”, and a stack-up consisting of 2 aluminium rings and 4130 wheel speed sensor plate as the inner race spacer. With sufficient care during machining, it is possible to create a controlled offset between the height of the step in the upright and the stack of rings.

When the assembly is torqued, the spacer stack will compress and further reduce the axial clearance between the bearings. The accumulative effects of the difference in spacer height as well as compression of the inner spacer stack will become the position preload of the hub bearings.

NSK specifies a range of axial clearances for their 79C series of bearings. To be within acceptable range for the operation of the bearings, both bearings should have minimally no positive clearance (7908 allows for 1 micron) and both should not exceed -22 and -25 microns of displacement respectively.

4. Outboard component stiffness testing

With all the suspension outboard components and arms mounted onto the monocoque, it is a good opportunity to test the toe and camber stiffness of the front and rear suspension.

Toe and camber stiffness are important to ensure that the suspension system maintains consistent geometry. Forces during cornering and braking will propagate from the tyres to the monocoque, and such forces were replicated in the stiffness test.

The setup below was created to test the real-life toe and camber stiffness of the suspension system. A stiffness jig was used to apply a load to suspension outboard, while the monocoque was weighed down and secured to the welding table via ratchet straps. Dial gauges were employed to track the displacement of the components under load as well as the monocoque itself. A weighing scale and a scissor jack were used to apply and record the load on the stiffness jig.

The loads are applied in 5N increments, up to a maximum of 30N. There are several important measurements to be made:

1. The displacement of the jig itself. The 2 ends of the jig were probed with respect to the welding table, which directly represent the toe / camber stiffness.

2. The displacement of the upright assembly. The upper ball joint and the toe ball joint were probed respectively, with respect to the welding table. This is to account for the compliance of the jig itself.

3. The displacement of the solid link ball joint on the damper clevis. This point was probed with respect to the monocoque, and its purpose is to account for the compliance in the damper clevis.

4. Various other points on the monocoque were probed. This is to ensure that the read outs from measurement 1 are not the result of the entire monocoque shifting. Additional care has been taken to securely fasten the monocoque to the welding table, but a small displacement of up to 0.02mm was still observed between the monocoque and welding table. There were runs where the value exceeded 0.1mm, and those measurements were discarded as the monocoque has shifted excessively.

A FEA simulation was also conducted to check the results of the real-life experiment, and the results are compared using the graphs below:

It is immediately obvious that the deformation in simulation is much smaller than that of the real-life testing. This is due to the compliance of various components that are otherwise modelled as solid in the FEA. Such components include PTFE lining of the rod ends, wheel hub bearings with free play and even the monocoque itself.

Perhaps more crucial than the degree of deformation is the linearity of it. Previous stiffness test managed to produce linear results when loading / unloading, but the gradient of the stiffness curve will defer drastically. This was attributed to hysteresis within the system, and the main reason for deploying additional dial gauges in the R26e test. With the additional ratchet straps in place and the position of the monocoque closely monitored, much more consistent stiffness graphs were obtained.

This information can aid in identifying the ideal alignment for the suspension. Knowing the correlation between load and geometry change allows us to pre-emptively adjust the toe and camber, such that deviation from the ideal alignment can be minimized.

Appendix 1: Ackermann

Ackermann steering percentage is a measure of how well the vehicle’s steering follows the Ackermann steering geometry. 100 percent Ackerman takes place when all of the wheels on the vehicles travel on paths that are concentric to the sane point without any of the wheels scrubbing. This assumes that there is very low slip angle at all wheels.

1. Introduction

The interim report concluded during the manufacturing phase of the 2026 season, where all the components have been designed and sent out to machine shops. At the time of writing, R26e has been assembled and basic functionality test are performed.

Shakedown of R26e was scheduled to take place in the second week of February, 2026. Due to unforeseen delays during the CNY period, the actual shakedown was postponed to March 28th, 2026. The team will continue to stress test and fine-tune the car until the competition in June 2026.

This report will pick up from the interim report and document the installation and validation of the various components. The scope covers the installation of brake hydraulics, hub bearings and preloads, suspension toe and camber stiffness, as well as documentation of manufacturing techniques.

Due to time constraints, it was not possible to validate the functionality of some components. The priority is to ensure reliability of the core vehicle systems, then move onto other systems that are not mission-critical.

The following content will also serve as documentation for the suspension juniors to gain a head start in next the next season, where they will explore designs of their own.


2. Brakes

2.1. Plumbing and installation

Brake lines were manufactured and installed onto the car. R26e utilizes 3AN stainless steel braided lines for its brake system. Stainless steel braided lines are both extremely durable and rigid, which helps to ensure maximum effectiveness of the braking system. Softer rubber lines are easier to route, but they can expand under pressure and lead to a soft pedal. A combination of direct thread and banjo hose ends were used depending on the location and accessibility of the connection. The BOM is as follows:

The addition of dry ice cooling containers presented a unique challenge for routing the brake lines. The usual path to the rear of the chassis has been blocked off. The shutoff solenoid also had to be mounted in a location that is both well protected and easy to access for brakes bleeding, with the pressure sensor mounted further downstream instead of at the pedal box. This was to monitor the actual pressure going to the callipers.

Hence, the brake lines were carefully laid out in CAD over multiple iterations to ensure no clashing with other components. A detailed brake line fabrication workflow will be illustrated in [Appendix 1] for reference.

For the brake rotors, brakes buttons and shims were used to secure them onto the hubs. The floating rotor system allows for outward thermal expansion when the rotor heats up. It also allows for small amounts of left-to-right movement of the rotors to account for the rigidly mounted callipers. Crucially, there is no radial play of the rotors relative to the hubs, as it can cause excessive vibrations and lead to the eventual failure of the components.

In this use case, brake buttons should be treated as a wear item and inspected routinely for any irregularity. Should there be excessive play between the rotor and the hub, it could be a sign that the brake buttons have been eroded and therefore not fully supporting the rotor. The brake buttons should then be replaced as soon as possible. The previous season’s race car, R25e, covered a total of 400km before a noticeable rattle occurred within the brake system. It was found during inspection that the wear of the brake buttons created free play, allowing them to contact the brake callipers. Fortunately, the incident did not cause catastrophic failure, but it drives home the importance of routine inspections and maintenance.


Once the brakes were installed, hydraulics were bled and friction surfaces were degreased and bedded in to ensure optimal braking response. Brake temperature sensors monitored the heating of each rotor. This is to ensure that there are no anomalies such as excessive runout or stuck pistons, which can lead to elevated temperatures and premature failure.

A motorcycle vacuum brake bleeder was graciously supplied by my colleague, Khai, and it was effective in priming the system with brake fluid. Due to the inverted master cylinder setup, it was very difficult to initial the bleeding process as fluid could not enter the chamber of the master cylinder by gravity. The vacuum pump drew in the fluid by creating a vacuum on the calliper side, making the process much easier.

With a properly installed brake system, the pedal should feel firm and have minimal free play before the brakes engage. If there is substantial pedal travel even with just pressure applied by hand, it is indicative of air bubbles being trapped in the system.

A good practice is to ensure that the bleeder valve on the calliper is at the highest position when bleeding the brakes. This can be achieved by removing the calliper from the upright and slotting a spacer plate between the brake pads (usually an acrylic plate of 4mm thickness), then holding the calliper with the bleeder valve facing up.

It is also common to have stubborn air bubbles that are difficult to remove. Vibrations applied to the brake lines can help. Alternatively, proper openning and closing the bleeder valves can also shock the bubbles free if done fast enough, as brake fluid will start and stop flowing suddenly.

When rotating the hub by hand, it is common to feel some resistance as there will be residual pressure in the master cylinder preventing the pistons in the calliper from retracting. However, there should not be any intermittent resistance as that indicates misaligned rotor. The position of the rotor between the callipers should also be checked. R26e experienced uneven piston extension on the front callipers, and the pistons only equalized after re-bleeding the brakes and going through brakes bedding.

2.2. Bedding

Once the braking system was operational, brakes bedding was performed to prepare the friction interface between the brake pads and brake rotors. This is achieved by applying consistent moderate braking pressure at high speed, and releasing the brakes before the car comes to a full stop. Friction material will be evenly transferred from the pads to the rotors and the area where the pad contacts the rotor will turn into a dull grey colour. The reason to avoid holding the brake while stationary is that if the brake pad has been heated up, it will continue to transfer friction material to the rotor where there is contact. This will cause uneven build up on the rotor and cause inconsistent braking performance.

Temperature sensors were unfortunately inoperable during bedding, though discolouration on the outside edges of the rotors can also be observed as they turned to straw colour, indicating that the temperature of the rotors did reach approximately 220 C. There is no need to reach peak temperature during bedding, but there still needs to be sufficient heat for the process to take place.



At this point, it is important to avoid touching the brake rotors with bare hands, as grease can adversely affect friction generation and braking performance. It is also advisable to periodically clean the brake rotors and pads with brake cleaner to remove any contaminants. The thickness of the brake pads should also be monitored. When the friction material becomes too thin, the pads should be replaced and the brakes need to be re-bedded.

Periodically rebleeding the brakes is also advisable, as brake fluid naturally absorbs moisture. This can cause corrosion within the hydraulic system, and the water content may boil under heat and turn into steam, creating a spongy pedal and seriosuly compromising brake performance.

2.3. Shutoff valve and regen

R26e marks the first time in NUS FSAE where regenerative braking is planned to perform all of the braking on the rear axle. Previous implementations on R25e and R24e use a blend of hydraulic and regenerative braking. This is a simple and reliable approach, but the amount of energy recuperated through regen will be limited as the hydraulics can never be fully decoupled. With the shutoff valve installed, it is possible to cut off all fluid flow to the rear brakes and lean more heavily on regen. The logic to derive how much regen is needed is covered in [Appendix 2].

Another feature of the new regen system is electronic brake bias. Previously, the driver had to interact with a physical cable in order to adjust brake balance. This method is prone to failure as the adjuster cable had a tendency to become jammed and prevent the balance bar from threading smoothly. With the implementation of the shutoff valve, it is possible to control brake bias via a knob on the dashboard, making the adjustment more consistent, more reliable as well as more tactile.

The shutoff valve is meant to be rule compliant, as the valve will only be activated when the high voltage system is active. During the technical inspection brake test, the driver has to first engage the emergency stop button on the dashboard, which will prevent the valve from engaging and allow the friction brakes to function on all 4 wheels.

These features are being validated, as the core functions of the powertrain are still undergoing testing. For now, the physical balance bar is tuned to driver preference and secured in place with jam nut. The effectiveness of the setup will be studied as time goes on.



3. Hub bearings and preload

Wheel bearings were installed with grease on fit interfaces as recommended by our bearings sponsor, NSK. This is to prevent corrosion and subsequent damage to the uprights and hubs. The fits between the wheel bearings, uprights and hubs are transitional fit (k5 and H6).

A special consideration when using angular contact bearings is the preload. Angular contact bearings are almost exclusively used in tandem, and R26e uses a back-to-back configuration.

Preload refers to the assembly adjustment to the clearance of the bearing (typically negative clearance to keep the bearings stressed). Preload is important for maintaining rigidity during operation and ensuring that the rolling elements in the bearing do not slide. Failing to maintain proper preload will cause the rotating assembly to have poor running accuracy and experience premature wear.

To achieve preload, there are 2 common techniques:

1. Position preload. This technique involves the use of spacers on inner and outer races of the bearings, thus controlling the displacement between the inner and outer races.

2. Constant-pressure preload. This technique exerts pressure to push the inner race against the outer race by means of some spring elements.

The design of R26e’s upright falls under position preload, as it utilizes a step in the upright to act as the outer race “spacer”, and a stack-up consisting of 2 aluminium spacers and a 4130 wheel speed sensor plate as the inner race spacer. With sufficient care during machining, it is possible to create a controlled offset between the height of the step in the upright and the stack of rings.


When the assembly is torqued, the spacer stack will compress and further reduce the axial clearance between the bearings. The combined effects of the difference in spacer height as well as compression of the inner spacer stack will become the position preload of the hub bearings.

NSK specifies a range of axial clearances for their 79C series of bearings. To be within acceptable range for the operation of the bearings, both front (7908) and rear (7912) bearings should have minimally no positive clearance (7908 allows for 1 micron) and both should not exceed -22 and -25 microns of displacement respectively. The specific class of preload is less important, as the running accuracy of the assembly would be sufficient in all cases.

The required offset distance should be twice that of chosen tolerance, as the axial displacement will be shared amongst the 2 bearings. This means that if each bearing needs to be preloaded by 10 microns, the combined effects of hub and upright height difference and spacer stack compression should result in an axial displacement of 20 microns.

The torque value of 130Nm results in a clamping force of just over 14000N, which was fed into another simualtion where the compressive force is directly applied to the spacer stack to verify the displacement of the bearing inner races. At 130Nm of torque, the spacer stack will compress by -1.2008e-002 mm or 12.008 microns. The workflow of manufacturing the outboard components will then be to machine the upright first as it is much more complicated in geometry compared to the spacer stack. Through techniques like coordinate measuring machines (CMM) or even micrometre screw gauge, the thickness of the step in the upright can be obtained. Knowing that the spacer stack needs to create a specific offset according to the type of preload selected, it is then possible to machine the spacer stack to have an initial thickness that gives the correct offset after being compressed 12 microns, achieving the ideal preload on the bearings.

As for the rear wheel hub, each of the M6 bolt will contribute approximately 10000N of clamping force, which according to simulation will result in a compression of 5 microns. A similar process can be followed to then determine the correct spacer height at the rear.

A final consideration for preloading the front and rear wheel hubs is to ensure that the preloading mechanism does not become obstructed. In the case of the front hub, if the preload nut runs out of thread and bottoms out on the stub that holds the inner races of the bearings, it will not be able to effectively compress the spacer stack; for the rear hub, if the hub is too long, the preload plate will bottom out in a similar fashion.

The best way to prevent such issues from arising is to specify the appropriate length of the hubs during machining, such that the preload mechanism will not bottom out until the spacer is fully compressed. The length does not have to be exactly matching the compression of the spacer, but it needs to be at least equal or larger.



4. Outboard component stiffness testing

With all the suspension outboard components and arms mounted onto the monocoque, it is a good opportunity to test the toe and camber stiffness of the front and rear suspension.

Toe and camber stiffness are important to ensure that the suspension system maintains consistent geometry. Forces during cornering and braking will propagate from the tyres to the monocoque, and such forces were replicated in the stiffness test.

The setup below was created to test the real-life toe and camber stiffness of the suspension system. A stiffness jig was used to apply a load to suspension outboard, while the monocoque was weighed down and secured to the welding table via ratchet straps. Dial gauges were employed to track the displacement of the components under load as well as the monocoque itself. A weighing scale and a scissor jack were used to apply and record the load on the stiffness jig.


The loads were applied in 5N increments, up to a maximum of 30N. Upon reaching 30N, the load was then gradually stepped down in 5N increments to observe the linearity and repeatability of deflection. There are several important measurements to be made:

1. The displacement of the jig itself. The 2 ends of the jig were probed with respect to the welding table, which directly represent the toe / camber stiffness.

2. The displacement of the upright assembly. The upper ball joint and the toe ball joint were probed respectively, with respect to the welding table. This is to account for the compliance of the jig itself.

3. The displacement of the solid link ball joint on the damper clevis. This point was probed with respect to the monocoque, and its purpose is to account for the compliance in the damper clevis.

4. Various other points on the monocoque were probed. This is to ensure that the read outs from measurement 1 are not the result of the entire monocoque shifting. Additional care has been taken to securely fasten the monocoque to the welding table, but a small displacement of up to 0.02mm was still observed between the monocoque and welding table. There were runs where the value exceeded 0.1mm, and those measurements were discarded as the monocoque has shifted excessively.

A simulation was also setup to compare against the results from the real life testing. The simulation setup is similar to the quarter car FEA covered in the interim report, with the addition of the stiffness testing jig. Time steps were set up to mimic the loading during the real life testing. The results are as follows:





It was immediately obvious that the deformation in simulation is much smaller than that of the real-life testing. This was due to the compliance of various components that are otherwise modelled as solid in the FEA. Such components include PTFE lining of the rod ends, wheel hub bearings with free play and even the monocoque itself. The additional dial gauges served as a good sanity check, as small deflections could be observed along the load path.

Perhaps more crucial than the degree of deformation was the linearity of it. Some deviation could be observed, but this can be attributed to hysteresis within the system. Care has been taken to ensure the stability of the experiment, with additional dial gauges and ratchet straps deployed and the position of the monocoque closely monitored. A more secure fixture can definitely be devised, but it will come at the cost of complexity and ease of installation.

The load of 30N on the jig created 83.553Nm of moment onto the outboard assemblies. Some cross checking was required between OptimumK, the load calculator covered in the interim report and extrapolation. This corresponds to the following operating conditions:

Front suspension: 0.32G of cornering for camber stiffness and 1.44G of braking for toe stiffness. Deflection of 0.38096 degrees for camber and 1.0559 degrees for toe.

Rear suspension: 0.33G of cornering for camber stiffness and 0.51G of acceleration for toe stiffness. Deflection of 0.4879 degrees for camber and 0.73072 degrees for toe.

This information can aid in identifying the ideal alignment for the suspension. Knowing the correlation between load and geometry change allows us to preemptively adjust the toe and camber, such that deviation from the ideal alignment can be minimized. However, the measurements showed that the camber stiffness seemed to be especially low. If the results were to be used in setting up the car, more than 2 degrees of negative camber would be required on both the front and rear axles, which raises some doubt as to the accuracy of the experiment. The likely cause for the excessive deflection was identify to be the worn out rod ends on the solid links used to setup the stiffness test, which unfortunately could not be replaced.

In the end, the car was set to have -1 degree of camber all around and 0 degrees of toe. This is taking into account the natural camber and toe changes in pitch and roll. This setup will serve as the basis for further tunning on the skid pad and autocross, where laptime, tyre temperatue and driver feedback can be used to further fine tune the alignment.



5. Future works

The condition of all components will be closely monitored until competition. Due to the timeline of the project, R26e has only seen limited mileage and the reliability of various systems remains uncertain. In fact, at the time of writing, the car has yet to receive its full aerodynamic package and anti-roll bars.

At the current state, the powertrain of R26e is not ready to implement regen. Therefore, testing of the shutoff valve capability is limited to bench testing, where the valve is manually activated as the brakes are applied. It can be verified that the valve is able to hold pressure, but its effects on the amount of energy recuperated remains to be seen.

The stiffness test setup also has room for improvement, as the dial gauges at extreme ends of the monocoque still recorded some deflection despite the extra attention given to securing the entire setup. It might be worth looking into more rigid mounting setups and directly fastening the monocoque to the welding table to prevent movement. The worn out rod ends on the solid links also resulted in excessive camber compliance, which made it difficult to predict the behaviour of the outboard components.

Further fine tuning is also required to extract the maximum amount of performance from the car. Extensive testing will be required to determine the ideal settings for brake bias, tyre pressure, ARB rates, dampers, etc. As testing went on, it became clear that there is a lack of camber adjustment at the front and an excess of camber adjustment at the rear. This is because of sub-optimal geometry during design phase, and thickness of the camber shim at neutral alignment should be considered more seriously in the future.

FSAE is a process of continuous improvement. While many enhancements have been made to the design of the car, many more weaknesses have also been uncovered. The current timelines will see the car undergoing substantial testing during April, and the team is expected to fly to the US for competition in early June. There are limited opportunities to conduct test runs, and the team will have to make the best of the situation.

Appendix 1: Brake line manufacturing

Before any real-life manufacturing can begin, it is very important to perform a mock up in CAD to check for clearance. Minimal bend radius for 3AN stainless braided PTFE lines is 1.5” or 38.1mm, therefore proper planning is required to avoid pinching the lines. Be mindful of bend radius of the brake hoses and potential tie-down points along the desired route. Also avoid dangerous areas (e.g. rotating assemblies) and obey rules (e.g. no lines under exposed chain).

Step 1: Perform mock up using strings, copy the setup from CAD and make adjustments if necessary.

Step 2: Cut the brake line to length, using fresh cutting disk. Tape the cut area to avoid excessive fraying of the stainless steel braiding, this can become very troublesome to deal with later.

Step 3: Prepare the hose ends and heat shrink. The heat shrink is not for the protection of the brake lines, but rather for that of the surrounding components as the stainless steel braiding is abrasive.

Step 4: Slip the heat shrink and collars of the hose ends over the brake line. Extra caution is warranted as forgetting any of the components will make it extremely difficult to reinstall them later on.

The end of the hose should look like this after being cut, assuming that the tape was able to prevent fraying of the stainless steel braiding. This is the best time to slip on the heat shrink and the threaded collar as the outer diameter of the hose is still small.


The heat shrink and the threaded collars have been installed. In the process of doing so, the braiding became frayed but it is of no concern at this stage.

Step 5: Now that the threaded collar is in place, the stainless steel braiding needs to be expanded further to make room for the brass compression fitting. A flaring tool is used to displace the braiding.


The braiding would be expanded, allowing for the brass compression fitting to be pushed into palace. It is important to ensure that the inner PTFE tube is seated all the way into the brass compression fitting.

It is usually the case that the braiding covers too much of the compression fitting. This can prevent the threaded collar from lifting up high enough to engage the other side of the hose end, and may even dislodge the brass compression fitting in the process, leading to poor seal.

The excess braiding needs to be trimmed away evenly until about half of the taper on the compression fitting is exposed.

Once that is done, pull the threaded collar over the compression fitting while being careful to keep the fitting seated fully.

Thread in as much of the hose end by hand as possible before using a wrench to avoid cross threading. Once the threads have positively engaged, vice the threaded collar and tighten the hose end all the way using the closed end of the wrench to avoid stripping the hose end. The threads should either bottom out or become impossible to tighten further by hand, indicating the complete crush of the compression fitting.

Lastly, shrink the heat shrink and blow out the completed hose using brake cleaner and compressed air to remove debris that might have gotten trapped in the line during cutting.

Once the lines are installed, it is always a good idea to check for interference with other components. Zip ties and wire lugs will be your best friend, as we well as clever improvisations.



Appendix 2: Brake regen logic

The simplified logic of brake regen is described as follows:

This assumes that the front and rear brake pads have the same frictional coefficient (in reality front is 0.6 and rear is 0.56) and that the front and rear callipers have the same diameter (in reality front is 25mm and rear is 25.4mm).

Bias is a value set by the driver, controlled from the dashboard. The balance bar is set to a position that will allow the driver to lock up all 4 wheels under hard braking in order to pass the brake test component of the technical inspection. Afterwards, the jam nut on the bias bar is tightened and the physical brake bias is locked.

The sequence of a braking event is as follows:

1. Driver presses on the brake pedal, which causes both the front and rear line pressure to increase.

2. Once the front and rear line pressure crosses a certain threshold, it can be taken as the driver intends on activating the brakes and did not accidentally step on the brake pedal. A number of checks will be performed, such as the speed of the vehicle and the state of charge of the tractive battery. Once the conditions are met, power will be sent to the shutoff valve to activate the solenoid and cut off fluid flow to the rear brakes.

3. The rear line pressure will cease to increase regardless of how much pedal force is applied by the driver, while the front line pressure is still determined by how hard the driver steps on the brake pedal. The pressure sensors in both the front and rear brake lines will feed information to the ECU, which will perform the calculations and determine the correct amount of negative torque to send to the motor for regen braking.

4. Once the driver releases the brake pedal and pressure in the front line falls below the threshold, the brake shutoff valve is no longer powered and fluid can freely return to master cylinder.

5. In the event of an anomaly (i.e. brake over travel switch is activated or LV system is lost), the shutoff valve will not be activated at all and hydraulic pressure can be sent to both front and rear axles. The car will behave as if it has traditional hydraulic brakes.



Appendix 3: Hub Thread Simulation

Ansys transient structuctural simulation is used to investigate the appropriate torque for the preload nut as it involves large displacements of the bodies and varying load conditions. The only drawback is the long runtime of the simulation, as smaller substeps are used to ensure convergence. The final simulation took just over 3 days to complete.

Step 1: Create the geometry in Solidworks. A M36 thread was first created on the hub. Using the cavity funcrion, a corresponding internal thread was the created on the nut, with a small offset to ensure that the 2 parts can slide. Small sharp edges were trimmed away to prevent meshing issues.

Step 2: The geometry was loaded into Ansys static structural, and the simulation was split into 14 steps with each step applying 10Nm of additional torque. Ensure that mesh defeaturing is off as we do not want to cause the thread surfaces to become distorted. This is also the reason for trimming away small and sharp edges in CAD.
